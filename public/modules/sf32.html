Non-Parametric Tests

When the data breaks normality assumptions

You're analyzing an A/B test on order value with only 300 users per group and a heavily right-skewed distribution. A t-test assumes approximate normality, but you're far from it at this sample size. Bootstrap is one option — non-parametric tests are another. Non-parametric tests make no assumptions about the underlying distribution; they work by ranking the data rather than using raw values. Mann-Whitney U is the right tool when samples are small, data is ordinal, or distributions are heavily skewed — but be precise about what it tests. Its null hypothesis is equal DISTRIBUTIONS, not equal medians: it asks whether one group tends to produce larger values than the other (stochastic dominance), not whether the means or medians differ. You can read a significant result as a difference in medians ONLY if you add a location-shift assumption (the two distributions have the same shape, just shifted). And note what it does not give you: it does not test the mean order value that the business usually reports.

How this connects to experimentation: Revenue metrics in A/B tests are often right-skewed with heavy tails (a few large orders dominate). For small samples, the CLT approximation fails. Non-parametric tests or bootstrap CIs are the correct alternatives when you cannot rely on normality.

Key takeaways

  • Non-parametric tests make no distributional assumption — they rank rather than use raw values.
  • Mann-Whitney U fits small, ordinal, or heavily skewed samples.
  • Its null is equal distributions, not equal medians — read it as a median difference only under a location-shift assumption, and it does not test the mean.
