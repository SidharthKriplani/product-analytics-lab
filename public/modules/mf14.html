Cohort Metrics and Retention Curves

Reading retention tables and spotting cohort-level shifts

Your overall D7 retention is 32% — same as last month. But the January cohort retained at 38% and the February cohort at 26%. The aggregate stayed flat because the January cohort is larger and masks the February decline. Retention tables decompose a single headline number into the cohort-level story: which group of users, acquired when, is retaining at what rate over time? Without this view, you will miss real product regressions that the aggregate smooths away.

How this connects to experimentation: In A/B tests, retention is one of the most common primary or guardrail metrics. But measuring retention requires choosing a cohort window and a measurement window. A test that shows +2% D7 retention may show -1% D30 retention — the intervention changed short-term behavior but not long-term habit formation.

Key takeaways

  • A flat aggregate D7 can hide a larger old cohort masking a newer cohort's decline.
  • Retention tables show which cohort, acquired when, retains at what rate over time.
  • +2% D7 can be -1% D30 — choose your cohort and measurement windows deliberately.
