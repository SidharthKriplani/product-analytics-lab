Proxy Design: What to Do When You Don't Have the Exact Data

"We don't have that data" is never a complete answer. Senior analysts propose proxies with stated limitations. Here's how to do it under interview pressure.

"We don't have that data" is a dead end. It's technically true in many situations — lifetime value isn't observable until a user leaves, 12-month retention takes 12 months, and true engagement quality requires surveys you haven't run. But treating unavailable data as a blocker signals analytical passivity. Senior analysts respond differently: they name what they can't observe, then design a proxy they can observe — and label it explicitly as a proxy so everyone knows what they're working with.

The Five-Step Proxy Design Process

Step 1: Name the true outcome. Be precise — not "engagement" but "probability a user is still active at month 12." Step 2: Identify correlated leading indicators. What behaviors in the first 30 days predict 12-month outcomes? Session frequency, feature adoption depth, invite rate, profile completion with profile usage? Step 3: Test the correlation on historical data. Pull cohorts where you do have 12-month data and check which early behaviors predict long-run outcomes. Step 4: Pick the proxy with the highest correlation and fastest signal. If D30 login frequency correlates at r=0.74 with 12-month retention and you can observe it 11 months earlier, that's your proxy. Step 5: Label it as a proxy in every communication — never present it as the real thing.

Designing a proxy for 12-month LTV using D30 behavior on Crafted: True outcome: 12-month LTV (total buyer GMV over first year).
Problem: Only observable for cohorts that are 12+ months old.

Leading indicators tested on 6 historical cohorts:
- D30 purchase count → correlation with 12-month LTV: r = 0.71
- D30 unique seller interactions → r = 0.68
- D30 save-to-wishlist count → r = 0.43 (weaker signal)
- Whether user left a review in first 30 days → r = 0.61

Best proxy: D30 purchase count (fastest + strongest signal).
Proxy label: "D30 purchase count is used as a proxy for 12-month LTV. Historical correlation: r = 0.71 across 6 cohorts (n=3,200 users/cohort). We will validate against actual 12-month LTV for the Jan cohort in Q4."

This proxy lets the team run experiments with 30-day readouts rather than 12-month wait times.
Key insight: Proxy metrics decouple when the product changes significantly. A D30 purchase count proxy trained on Crafted's pre-recommendation-engine behavior may not hold after you ship a personalization algorithm that changes how users discover products. Re-validate proxy correlations after major product changes — don't assume the historical relationship persists.

In interviews, the proxy design question often sounds like: "We want to improve user quality — how would you measure that?" The trap is answering with a vague metric like "engagement score." The strong answer names the true outcome (long-term retention or revenue), explains why it's not directly observable in the interview timeframe, then designs a specific proxy with a stated validation plan. The validation plan — "I'd back-test this proxy on 6 historical cohorts and check whether the correlation holds" — is the detail that separates a strong answer from a vague one.
