Statistical Significance vs Practical Significance: The Distinction That Changes Decisions

A 0.01% CVR lift can be statistically significant at scale. It is almost never practically significant. Knowing which you're looking at prevents bad ship decisions.

Statistical significance answers the question: is this effect real, or could it be noise? Practical significance answers a different question: is this effect large enough to care about? At sufficient scale, almost any non-zero effect becomes statistically significant. With 10 million users, you can detect a 0.002% CVR lift with p < 0.05. That lift is real. It is almost certainly not worth the engineering cost, the maintenance burden, or the product complexity it introduces. Confusing these two questions produces ship decisions that are analytically defensible but commercially indefensible.

Minimum Detectable Effect as the Right Frame

The practical significance question should be answered before the experiment runs, not after. The tool is the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE): the smallest lift that would meaningfully change a business decision. To set the MDE, ask: if the treatment produces a lift smaller than X, would we ship it? If the answer is no — because the lift doesn't justify the engineering cost, or the effect is too small to compound into meaningful revenue — then X is your practical significance threshold. You set the MDE, run the power calculation with that MDE, and design the experiment accordingly. When the results come in, statistical significance only matters if the observed lift exceeds the MDE.

Crafted CVR: what lift size justifies the engineering cost?: Context: Crafted marketplace is considering a checkout redesign. Engineering estimate: 3 weeks (1 engineer).
Opportunity cost: 3 weeks × $8,000 engineer week = ~$24,000 in eng cost.
Monthly checkout volume: 180,000 transactions.
AOV: $58.

Break-even lift calculation:
$24,000 / ($58 × 180,000) = 0.23% absolute CVR lift to cover eng cost in month 1.
For a 12-month payback: $24,000 / 12 = $2,000/month needed → 0.019% absolute CVR lift.

MDE recommendation: Set MDE at 0.15% absolute (a conservative threshold above the break-even). Any detected lift below 0.15% is real but practically irrelevant — the feature doesn't pay for itself. Any lift above 0.15% justifies the investment.

Result: If p < 0.05 but lift = 0.04%, this is statistically significant and practically irrelevant. Do not ship.

The Communication Problem

The hardest part of this distinction is communicating it to stakeholders who see "statistically significant" and conclude "ship it." The framing that works: "The result is statistically significant, which means the effect is real and not noise. However, the observed lift is 0.04%, which at our volume translates to approximately $4,200/month in incremental revenue. The engineering cost to maintain this feature is estimated at X. On a purely financial basis, the feature doesn't pay for itself within a reasonable horizon. I'd recommend not shipping." This converts the statistical result into business terms and frames the decision correctly.

The two questions to answer for every experiment result: 1. Is the effect real? (Statistical significance — p-value and confidence interval) 2. Is the effect large enough to matter? (Practical significance — does the lift exceed the pre-set MDE?) Both must be yes to ship. Statistical significance alone is not sufficient.
